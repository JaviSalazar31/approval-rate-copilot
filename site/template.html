Approval Rate Copilot

An AI analyst for a payments team. Ask it why card approvals dropped and it comes back with the cause and the cost, plus a recommended next step. Each number is backed by SQL you can open.

A personal product case on synthetic data for a fictional payment orchestrator in Mexico, Colombia and Brazil. A Databricks pipeline cleans four inconsistent data feeds, and a Genie agent and an AI/BI dashboard sit on top, all deployed on Databricks Free Edition. Its evaluation uses {{eval.total}} benchmark questions and {{eval.heldout_questions}} held-out ones, in seven sets.

The problem

The feeds disagree

A payment orchestrator earns money only when payments are approved. When approval drops, the payments team needs to know quickly what it is costing and why, so it can decide what to change.

To answer, someone has to join the orchestrator log with three acquirer feeds that use different time zones, amount formats, column names and codes for the same issuer answer. The pipeline turns all of that into one canonical status per attempt, through a versioned map that records which rule classified each decline.

One row of that map, the one marked in the table, is the latent cause of the incident below.

Two causes stacked

Acquirer B approves Colombian debit less often than A, and a decline-code map released a week earlier had stopped B's "do not honor" declines from being retried on a second acquirer. The copilot does not split the drop between the two causes, so I estimated the split myself. Had B's {{incident.b05_window_failures|int}} "do not honor" declines been retried and recovered at B's Colombian recovery rate in the 28 days before the bug ({{incident.b05_co_recovery_rate|pct0}}), about {{incident.b05_lost_in_window|int}} more payments would have been approved: about {{incident.drop_from_missing_retries_pp|abs1}} points ({{incident.missing_retries_low_pp|abs1}}–{{incident.missing_retries_high_pp|abs1}} with other reasonable rates). B's weaker approval explains the remaining {{incident.drop_from_weaker_acquirer_pp|abs1}}.

The warning came six days early

Retries of B's "do not honor" declines fell from 100% to {{incident.b05_first_day_cascade_rate|pct0}} on 6 August, the day the new map went live, and to 0% from the next day. Daily approval barely moved, so a dashboard that watches only outcomes stayed green. A cascade alert that watches the retry mechanism itself flagged it in the next morning's run and in every daily run until the fix, but in the scenario nobody owned it, so nobody acted. Over those nine days, Mexico and Colombia lost about {{incident.b05_lost_recoveries|round10}} approvals (about USD {{incident.b05_lost_recoveries_usd|k0}} of approved volume) that a retry would normally have recovered, most of them before the visible incident.

Checked against a control group

The copilot compares the window with the same segment's previous 14 days, which holds only if nothing else moved. The copilot

One question, five checks

The copilot is a Databricks Genie agent on the gold tables. I chose to put the diagnostic method in five Unity Catalog SQL functions. The model decides what to check, runs those functions and its own SQL when it needs to, and explains the result. Every query appears under the answer.

Evaluation

How I tested it

{{eval.total}} questions in four groups, each with a scoring rule: 16 with ground-truth SQL and 4 guardrails with an expected behavior. Offline, a Claude model (Sonnet) played the copilot with the same instructions, tables and functions as the Genie agent; a test harness ran its SQL on the pipeline output, and a separate model (Claude Opus) graded every answer, including whether each number came from a query. Each run saw only its instructions and its own query results. The same questions then ran on the deployed Genie agent (the G columns). The repository has every answer, query and verdict.

Limits

After every fix the twenty benchmark questions ran again, so they show that the fixes work on these cases. The held-out sets check whether the fixes generalize: {{eval.heldout_total}} questions asked once, then six sets of {{eval.heldout2_total}} asked {{eval.heldout2_times}} each, and the repeated samples show how much one sample hides: {{eval.heldout2_mixed}} of the second set's questions, {{eval.heldout3_mixed}} of the third's, {{eval.heldout4_mixed}} of the fourth's, {{eval.heldout5_mixed}} of the fifth's, {{eval.heldout6_mixed}} of the sixth's and {{eval.heldout7_mixed}} of the seventh's passed in some samples and failed in others. Each set is new and was committed to the repository before its run. On Genie, B1 and C3 also ran three times in runs 2 to {{eval.genie_runs}}, and reworded variants of failed questions passed {{eval.genie_variants}} in run 4. The judge is a model; its verdicts are published, and I overrule a verdict only to make it stricter. The offline model stood in for Genie and overstated it: {{eval.last_passed}} of {{eval.total}} offline against {{eval.genie_first}} on the first Genie run.

Decisions

Product calls that shaped it

ADR-06

Answers come from SQL queries

The questions are quantitative, so the answers have to be exact and auditable. Tested SQL functions hold the diagnostic method, and the model picks which ones to run and adds its own SQL when it needs to.

ADR-08

Alert on the safety net too

A broken retry barely moves daily approval. An alert on the retry rate itself flagged the map bug in its first daily run, six days before the visible incident. The retry policy must also respect the card networks' reattempt rules.

ADR-01

A versioned decline-code map

Every attempt keeps the map version that classified it. That lets the copilot say "05 was soft under v2.2 and hard under v2.3".

ADR-07

Volume-aware anomaly detection

A day is flagged only if the drop is large in points, unusual for its history and unlikely for its volume. On the 90 days used to tune it, it flagged one day: the planted incident. A three-hour issuer outage needs hourly detection.

ADR-12

Teach the agent with structure

On Genie, short text rules held less well than they had offline. Through the seventh release, each fix went into the most structured place that could carry it: a function's output or parameters, an example query, a description. The benchmark went from {{eval.genie_first}} to {{eval.genie_round3}} of {{eval.total}} in three rounds, and the seventh release, in which the functions return more of the answer, passed {{eval.genie_run7}}. The eighth, with rules and examples in the text, passed {{eval.genie_run8}}, and chat mode's direct function calls did not follow those rules. The ninth put the ranking and the rate in the function's parameters, which chat mode fills in: C4 passed for the first time, C2 failed for the first time once chat mode answered it with the same function, and the ninth passed {{eval.genie_run9}}. The tenth, the last release, gave every function the incident date and passed {{eval.genie_passed}}, though chat mode then filled in that date where no incident was named.

ADR-09

Evaluate before rollout

A fixed question set with ground truth runs before every change to instructions, functions or tables. Without a fixed set, there is no way to tell whether instructions that look better make the answers worse.

The system

From raw feeds to clean tables

A Lakeflow pipeline, Unity Catalog SQL functions, an AI/BI dashboard and a Genie agent were deployed on Databricks Free Edition on 29 October 2026. The same SQL also runs on local Spark, where every number on this page was first computed; on Databricks the generated files match byte for byte, and the pipeline quarantines the same {{headline.quarantined|int}} rows. Since 4 November 2026 one job runs the whole sequence, from landing the files to the governance checks. dbt builds and tests the gold layer as a task of that job, three Unity Catalog metric views hold the approval, volume, decline and funnel definitions that the agent reads, two of them also read by dashboard datasets, and since 5 November the workspace deploys from GitHub Actions with an Asset Bundle. A separate job checks that late, out-of-order acquirer status events are applied in the acquirer's sequence with AUTO CDC, in a schema the copilot never reads.

Checks before the pipeline, alerts after it

Each raw feed has a data contract. Before the pipeline runs, a check stops the run on a missing or empty file, a changed column, values that break their format in more than 1% of a column, a repeated payment ID or a volume far from the previous week's, an acquirer file that does not reconcile with the payments routed to it, or any value that looks like a full card number. Single bad rows still go to quarantine. One Databricks job runs every step in order, and CI on GitHub Actions runs the tests, the whole pipeline and dbt on every push to main.

The approval and retry alerts watch payments that reached an acquirer, so a loss before that point does not move them. A third alert forecasts approved payments with weekday and payday terms. With every method set to the same false-alert rate on the reference data, it caught {{analysis.forecast_20|pct0}} of the days cut by 20% in three more synthetic datasets, against {{analysis.same_weekday_20|pct0}} for a same-weekday mean and {{analysis.trailing_mean_20|pct0}} for a trailing 28-day mean. Those datasets share the generator's calendar, so they test new noise, not holidays or promotions. None of the planted incidents is that large, so the rate alerts remain the ones that catch them.

A change guardrail scores every production change hour by hour for its first day, against the segment's 14 days before it. The daily job rebuilds it; running it every hour after a change is the next step. On the reference data it fired {{analysis.guardrail_hours|int}} hours after routing v14 went live, when {{analysis.guardrail_lost_at_fire|round0}} approvals and USD {{analysis.guardrail_usd_at_fire|round0}} of approved volume were gone against USD {{analysis.guardrail_fees_at_fire|round0}} of fees saved. Rolled back an hour after the alert, the incident would have cost {{analysis.guardrail_lost_rolled_back|round0}} approvals instead of {{analysis.guardrail_lost_actual|round0}}. In all four datasets it fired {{analysis.guardrail_hours_min|int}} to {{analysis.guardrail_hours_max|int}} hours after that change, on no other change, and in at most {{analysis.guardrail_quiet_fired_max|int}} of {{analysis.guardrail_quiet_windows|int}} quiet 24-hour windows each. It missed the decline-map change of 6 August, which cut Mexican domestic debit by {{analysis.guardrail_map_pp|abs1}} points over its first day (the retry alert flagged it the next morning), and it does not see payments lost to risk blocks before they reach an acquirer. I set its threshold after seeing these runs.

Next

What I would build next

  1. Pilot on real data with three analysts for four weeks, after short interviews, and measure time to diagnosis against today's process, with a log of wrong or missing answers that starts from what run 10 and the seventh held-out set left open.
  2. Run the change guardrail every hour after a change, and ramp routing changes from 10% to 50% to 100% of traffic, with the guardrail judging each step and its rollback proposal going to the change owner.
  1. Add hourly detection on every segment for drops that come from outside, like the three-hour maintenance of an issuer on 21 July, which no daily alert sees.
  2. Give every alert a named owner and a runbook entry. In the scenario, the retry alert fired nine days in a row and nobody acted.
